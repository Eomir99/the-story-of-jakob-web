So, what was the point of all this?

I have been playing games for as long as I can remember. One of my earliest memories is sitting in the Age of Empires III scenario editor, building worlds before I really understood what I was doing.

But over time, my interest shifted from simply playing games to understanding the industry behind them.

I became fascinated by the CS skin economy, by the business models behind successful games, and by questions that seemed surprisingly difficult to answer: How can a game that appears to do everything right still fail? How can a small indie studio outperform a AAA production? How do you monetize a game effectively without turning the player base against you?

Eventually, those questions became more interesting to me than simply playing the games.

So when it was time to write my bachelor's thesis, the subject was obvious. I studied how video game companies adapt their products to international markets, interviewing ten people working in and around Nordic game studios and analysing the results through the integration-responsiveness framework and localization theory.

The biggest thing I learned was not that I understood the industry.

It was how much more complex it was than I had assumed.

Talking to people working in the industry challenged several of my own assumptions, gave me a much deeper understanding of how game companies operate, and made me even more certain that this is the industry I want to build my career in.

Which brings us back to this game.

I wanted my application to demonstrate that determination rather than simply claim it.

I am not a programmer. I am not an animator. A week ago, I had never built a game like this.

But I had an idea, access to AI tools, and a willingness to figure it out.

So I built it anyway.

I used AI to help me code, create and iterate on assets, solve problems, test ideas and turn a concept into the game you just played. The technical work was new to me. Directing the process, deciding what worked, questioning what did not, and continuously improving the result was not.

That is what I hope to bring to your team: a genuine understanding of and fascination with the games industry, a business background, curiosity, and the determination to learn whatever I need to turn an idea into something real.

The thesis

The strategic importance of product adaptation in the video game industry

A case study based on Nordic videogame companies

Bachelor thesis, University of Gothenburg, Spring 2026. Written with Axel Abrahamsson. Supervisor: Niklas Åkerman.

Question: How do video game firms strategically approach and implement product adaptation within international markets?

Method: Qualitative, abductive. Ten semi-structured interviews with industry professionals connected to Nordic studios and the wider international industry, analysed through the integration-responsiveness framework and localization theory.

What it found:

  • Firms almost never redesign a game for a region. They hold a standardised core and adapt selectively — localization, monetization, regional marketing, platform implementation, limited content changes.
  • Localization increasingly operates as an integrated strategic capability rather than a post-production translation step.
  • Regulatory and institutional adaptation is a first-order concern: censorship, age ratings, data protection, platform policy, monetization rules. China is the most institutionally complex market studied.
  • Because games keep evolving after release, adaptation is a continuous operational process rather than a market-entry activity.

About this game

The Story of Jakob is a short, browser-based interactive CV, built over roughly one week as an experiment in how far I could take an idea using AI tools.

The concept, creative direction, game design and the vast majority of the playtesting were done by me. That included deciding what the game should communicate, designing the progression and encounters, sketching scenes, directing the visual style, reviewing outputs and repeatedly iterating on what worked and what did not.

And yes, the sketches were made in PowerPoint. Very sophisticated indeed. Every comic and most major in-game sequences started there before being turned into finished assets.

The project was heavily AI-assisted:

  • Visual assets: Generated with ChatGPT, with some assets manually edited or adjusted in Aseprite.
  • Code: Roughly 80% written with Claude Code and 20% with OpenAI Codex, under my direction and iteration.
  • Audio: Generated locally using Stable Audio Open Small.
  • Design & direction: Almost entirely my own — from the overall concept and narrative to level flow, mechanics, asset selection and playtesting.
  • Hosting: The finished game is hosted through GitHub Pages.

I did not start this project as a programmer, animator or game developer. The point was never to pretend otherwise.

The interesting part, to me, was seeing whether I could use the tools available to turn a fairly ambitious idea into a coherent, playable finished product, while still making the creative and product decisions myself.
